# Quiet Planting for k-SAT, Multiple Solutions of Arbitrary Geometry

## Abstract

Recent work on "quiet planting" in combinatorial optimization aims to generate instances with a hidden solution that is hard to recover, typically by making the planted distribution statistically indistinguishable from uniform for specific algorithms, such as statistical queries. A prominent example is planted k-SAT, where O(n^{k/2}) clauses can be planted while maintaining indistinguishability from uniform instances, evidenced by prior hardness results which also align with findings in SAT refutation. Despite extensive research and practical use in benchmarking SAT solvers, the challenge of quietly planting multiple solutions while preserving hardness has remained an open problem.

This work initiates the study of quiet planting with an arbitrary number of solutions, proposing the first method to construct quiet planting distributions for k-SAT formulas that accommodate more than one solution. We provide statistical query lower bounds for distinguishing these planted instances from uniform ones, and our method allows for planting solutions with arbitrary geometric relationships, including varying Hamming distances. A key innovation facilitating multiple solutions is the ability to incorporate arbitrary correlations between variable selection in clauses and their negation patterns, departing from prior approaches. We also investigate the worst-case complexity of SAT by showing the difficulty in distinguishing satisfiable instances with numerous solutions from unsatisfiable ones, addressing an open problem of Hsieh, Mohanty, and Xu (CCC'22). From a technical standpoint, we generalize the concept of (r − 1)-wise uniformness in clause distributions, proving hardness holds if the marginal distribution over negation patterns is (r − 1)-wise uniform, and reveal a connection to binary linear codes, demonstrating how a [k, t, r] code can guide the planting of up to 2^t − 1 solutions on k variables with (r − 1)-wise uniform negation distributions.

## TL;DR

This paper is the formal, statistical-query (SQ) version of quiet planting for k-SAT, extended to **many planted solutions with prescribed mutual geometry**. Pick a binary linear code [k, t, r]. Let the clause's sign-pattern distribution depend on which variables the clause picks. Then up to 2^t − 1 solutions, which form a random XOR-rotation of any prescribed set A, can be planted while the instance stays SQ-indistinguishable from uniform k-SAT up to about n^{r/2} clauses. The caveat that matters most: SQ-quiet does not mean hard to solve. The authors note the construction falls to Gaussian elimination unless noise is added.

## Problem

Naively planted k-SAT is "solvable in linear time … by a majority vote" over literal polarities (§1). Quiet planting à la Feldman et al. (2015a) handles **one** planted solution, effectively via planted k-XOR-SAT. Planting **multiple** solutions while keeping indistinguishability was open. Earlier multi-solution work was either experimental or limited to "up to two solutions or in hypothesis testing settings", which includes [[achlioptas-jia-moore-2004-hiding-assignments]] (§7). Separately, Hsieh–Mohanty–Xu (CCC'22) asked how hard it is to distinguish unsatisfiable formulas from formulas with many solutions.

## Approach

- **Clause model (§2).** A clause is (I, x): an ordered k-tuple of variables I and a sign pattern x ∈ {0,1}^k. The prior construction samples I uniformly and x from a single Q that excludes the all-ones pattern, which plants σ via (I, σ[I] ⊕ x) (§3).
- **Obstruction (Prop. 2).** With a single Q independent of I, "there is no σ′ ∈ {0,1}^n having Hamming distance k to σ that is satisfied by every clause". So I-independent sign distributions cannot plant far-apart solutions.
- **New idea.** Let the sign-pattern distribution $Q_I$ depend on I. Use a [k, t, r] binary linear code's generator matrix to build $Q_I$ so that every pattern in its support satisfies all assignments in A, while any r − 1 coordinates are uniform (§5, Thm 4).
- **Hardness transfer (§4, Thm 3).** (r − 1)-wise uniformity (generalised to allow dependence between variable choice and negations) implies a large statistical dimension, hence SQ lower bounds via Feldman et al. (2015a, Thm 3.4).
- **Arbitrary geometry.** An exact prescribed A cannot be hidden, because a test could check those very points. The planted set is therefore {σ ⊕ τ : σ ∈ A} for one random τ per distribution, which preserves all pairwise XOR differences (§3).
- **Worst case (§6).** ETH-based hardness for SAT instances that are either unsatisfiable or have very many solutions.

## Key result

1. **Theorem 1 (main).** Assume a binary linear code [k, t, r] exists and L < 2^t. Let A be any set of at most L assignments. Then there is a family D of clause distributions such that (1) $\mathrm{SDN}\big(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{D}, U), \frac{c(\log q)^{r/2}}{n^{r/2}}\big) \ge q$ for any q ≥ 1, where c is a constant, and (2) for each Q ∈ D there is τ ∈ {0,1}^n such that every clause C ∼ Q satisfies all σ ⊕ τ for σ ∈ A with probability 1 (§3). Reading: "any randomly chosen distribution from D remains indistinguishable from the uniform distribution over k-clauses up to n^{r/2} clauses", and "any efficient algorithm for detecting the existence of our planted solutions requires at least Ω(n^{r/2}) clauses."
2. **Tightness check.** The repetition code [k, 1, k] gives single-solution planting that is quiet up to n^{k/2} clauses. This recovers Feldman et al. (2015a) and is equivalent to planted k-XOR-SAT (§3).
3. **Exponentially many solutions.** By Gilbert–Varshamov, "planting less than 2^{(1−H(δ))k} solutions is quiet up to n^{δk/2} clauses" (§3). Theorem 5: for 0 ≤ δ₀ ≤ 1/2 and |A| ≤ 2^{k(1−H(δ₀))} − 1 there is a (δ₀k − 1)-wise uniform distribution over k-clauses. So "a linear reduction in complexity allows an exponential expansion in the set of assignments" (§5).
4. **Theorem 3.** For every (r − 1)-wise uniform Q over $X_k$ there is c > 0, depending on k, with $\mathrm{SDN}(\mathcal{B}(D_Q, U), c(\log q)^{r/2}/n^{r/2}) \ge q$, where $D_Q$ is the set of XOR-rotations of Q. This generalises Thm 3.5 of Feldman et al. (2015a) (§4).
5. **Theorem 4.** If a [k, t, r] code exists, then for any k variables I and any |A| ≤ 2^t − 1 there is $Q_I$ whose support clauses are satisfied by A and whose marginal on any r − 1 coordinates is uniform (§5).
6. **Theorem 6 (worst case).** "Assuming the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), there is no polynomial-time algorithm for solving SAT(n, 2^{n−log^{1+c}(n)}) for any c > 0, even when it is guaranteed that the input is (7/8 + ϵ)-satisfiable." The exponent is n − (log n)^{1+c}, as confirmed by the Appendix D reduction from SAT((log n)^{1+c}, 1). Here SAT(n, s) is the class of instances that are unsatisfiable or have at least s solutions. For contrast, SAT(n, 2^{n−log n}) is solved by random guessing, since each guess succeeds with probability 1/n (§6).
7. **Vulnerability, stated by the authors.** "instances constructed by both our method and the prior approach can be solved using Gaussian elimination-based methods; however, introducing a small amount of noise can mitigate this vulnerability." (§1, end of Contributions)

## Assumptions

- Hardness holds only in the **statistical-query model**: oracles VSTAT, MVSTAT and 1-MSTAT, with complexity = number of queries × cost per query (§2). The authors argue this covers "EM, MCMC, method of moments, simulated annealing, and convex optimization". It does not cover algebraic algorithms such as Gaussian elimination.
- Clauses are drawn i.i.d. from a fixed Q, and the number of clauses is below about n^{r/2}.
- A [k, t, r] binary linear code exists with the needed parameters.
- Theorem 6 is conditional on ETH.

## Limitations / scope

- **SQ-indistinguishability is not solver hardness.** The XOR structure that makes the distribution SQ-quiet also makes it linear-algebraically solvable. The noise fix is mentioned but neither analysed nor tested.
- No experiments: no solver runtimes, no comparison with [[achlioptas-jia-moore-2004-hiding-assignments]]-style 2-hidden formulas or with random k-SAT at threshold. The DARPA QuICC footnote is the only practical evidence.
- The planted set is a random XOR-rotation of A, not A itself, and other solutions besides the planted ones may exist.
- Extending to low-degree tests is left as "a promising future direction" (§1).

## Replication evidence

None yet (the paper is new). The single-solution special case reproduces Feldman et al. (2015a)'s bound (repetition code).

## Why this paper matters

It turns "quiet planting" from a physics heuristic for symmetric CSPs ([[krzakala-zdeborova-2009-quiet-planting]]) and a symmetrisation trick for two solutions ([[achlioptas-jia-moore-2004-hiding-assignments]]) into a theorem about a formal distinguisher class, for k-SAT, with control over the planted solution set. The structural insight is Proposition 2. Planting more than one far-apart solution *requires* the sign pattern to correlate with which variables were chosen. A planter that treats "which positions" and "which polarity" independently cannot plant far-apart solutions.

**Relevance to challenge-gen:** The sharpest lesson is the authors' own caveat. A planted ensemble can be provably indistinguishable from random for one class of algorithms and still be easy for another (Gaussian elimination here). For challenge-gen, a certified trivial word whose surface statistics match random words may still be easy for an algebraic method: for example, evaluation in B₀(2,5) or a nilpotent quotient, or kbmag rewriting that exploits the generator's structure. So hardness evidence must come from actual solver runs across families, not from distributional quietness alone. The paper also gives a template for planting *many* solutions with controlled geometry. The analogue for Andrews–Curtis would be many AC-trivialisation paths of prescribed relative structure.

## Open questions surfaced

- Does adding noise to the construction preserve the SQ lower bound while defeating Gaussian elimination? How much noise is needed, and does the planted set remain satisfying? Stated in §1, not answered.
- Extension to low-degree polynomial tests (§1).
- Empirical hardness for CDCL and local search of the multi-solution planted instances vs 2-hidden and random formulas at threshold. No experiments are reported.
- How large can the planted set be relative to n before SQ indistinguishability fails, beyond what Gilbert–Varshamov gives?
