# Learning the Word Problem: Geodesic Lengths and Cryptographic Applications

## Abstract

"The Word Problem has been a subject of intensive mathematical study for over a century, initially driving advances in combinatorial group theory and more recently emerging as a foundational hardness assumption in post-quantum cryptography (PQC). While generally undecidable, several families of infinite non-abelian groups exhibit solvable or algorithmically fast word problems, making them attractive platforms for cryptographic design. This paper introduces WPNet, a novel Graph Neural Network architecture capable of solving the Word Problem heuristically, which is demonstrated on the Baumslag-Solitar group $BS(1,2)$ and on an Artin group. By mapping unreduced words to dynamic graph structures, the model learns to cluster algebraically equivalent elements in a continuous embedding space, effectively identifying the geodesic representative of a word without executing discrete reduction steps. As an application, a model variant is developed that can predict the geodesic length of an unreduced word in both groups. To demonstrate the cryptographic severity of this structural leakage, WPNet is successfully deployed against the Wagner-Magyarik public-key cryptosystem."

## TL;DR

WPNet is a GNN that turns a word into a chain graph of 5-letter chunks (BiLSTM, then gated GINE message passing, then attention pooling). It is trained contrastively, so that words equal in the group embed close together. It is tested on $BS(1,2)$ and a random rank-101 Artin group with $m_{ij} \in \{2,3,\infty\}$. Trained on lengths up to 100, it separates equivalent from perturbed words at 99.93% mean accuracy up to length 5000 in $BS(1,2)$. It predicts geodesic length (80.30% exact overall for $BS(1,2)$) and breaks Wagner–Magyarik. For challenge generation, the key point is that its trivial words are made by "tangling": inserting free pairs and relators, at most 150 operations. So their area is bounded by the number of operations, and the exponential Dehn function cited as motivation is never exercised.

## Problem

Can a neural network solve the word problem, and predict geodesic length, heuristically at the word lengths used in group-based post-quantum cryptography (hundreds to thousands of letters)? And does that leak enough to break a word-problem cryptosystem (Wagner–Magyarik)? (§1)

## Approach

**Groups** (§2.1):
- $BS(1,2) = \langle a, b \mid bab^{-1}a^{-2}\rangle$. Its WP is in uniform TC⁰, but Gersten showed its Dehn function is exponential (§2.1.1).
- A random Artin group $A(\Gamma)$ on $x_0..x_{100}$. Each Coxeter-matrix entry is ∞ with probability 10%, and 2 or 3 with 45% each, then symmetrised by max. With the chosen seed: "44 pairs of generators having no relation, 1523 pairs of generators commuting and 3483 pairs of generators having a braid relation" (§2.1.2).

**Encoding** (§2.2): the word is split into 5-letter subwords starting at every even index. Each subword is a node; edges join neighbours (type 1) and nodes at distance 2 (type 2). For $BS(1,2)$, letters get 7-dim sinusoidal features. For $A(\Gamma)$, letters get learned embeddings ($V = 202$).

**Training-data generation**, which is the part that matters for challenge-gen:
- **Contrastive triplets** (§2.2.1): 8192 triplets per batch, regenerated every 5 epochs. Bases are "evenly partitioned into trivial identity bases and uniformly sampled random sequence bases". Anchor and Positive are built "by independently tangling the base word through $N_{ops}$ randomized algebraic operations (e.g., inserting trivial pairs or applying group relators)". "Identity bases receive moderate to massive tangling (up to 150 operations)". Negatives come from a mutated base (block permutation or truncation) tangled with the same $N_{ops}$, so that length cannot be used as a cue. Training lengths are $L = 20$ to $100$.
- **Geodesic-length labels** (§2.2.2): sample a random word and reduce it. $BS(1,2)$ uses a greedy length-decreasing Dehn-style heuristic, an upper bound, because GAP rewriting blows up. $A(\Gamma)$ uses "free reduction with continuous lexicographical sorting" (claimed confluent). The result is then re-tangled with $k = \min(\text{intensity}, \max(2, \lfloor L_{true}/5 \rfloor))$ operations. Test inputs get at most 3 tangling operations (§2.5.2).
- **Wagner–Magyarik data** (§2.2.3, §2.5.3): ciphertext $C = R \cdot P_b$, where $R$ is "a highly tangled string generated via random insertions of $aa^{-1}$, $bb^{-1}$, and the relator $bab^{-1}a^{-2}$" (Example 2).

**Hardness control**: none beyond word length and the count of tangling operations. Area or Dehn-function cost of the generated trivial words is neither measured nor targeted. (This is a Researcher observation; the paper does not discuss it.)

**Losses** (§2.4): InfoNCE with a false-negative mask and an "Identity Gravity Penalty" for embeddings; cross-entropy over $L_{max} + 1 = 201$ length classes; triplet margin loss for Wagner–Magyarik. AdamW, lr $10^{-4}$. Hardware: one Quadro RTX 8000 plus RTX 5080 (§2.6).

**Embedding / equality (Table 2, §3.1)**, accuracy with margin $s_{pos} - s_{neg} > 0.2$:

"The model achieves a mean classification accuracy of 99.93% when considering words of length up to 5000. It should be emphasized here that the model only saw words of length up to 100 in training." Note that the evaluation negatives are positives with "25% to 50% of the letters" scrambled (§2.5.1), which is a coarse perturbation.

**Geodesic length (Table 3, §3.2)**: $BS(1,2)$ "global accuracy of 80.30% (MAE = 0.26)". Accuracy is above 90% on short words and 57% for lengths 180–200. Generalisation above length 200 is "very poor", which is expected for a classifier. In $BS(1,2)$, geodesics are "typically 10% to 20% of the original input length". The $A(\Gamma)$ parameters were chosen "to yield a shrinkage of approximately 20%".

**Wagner–Magyarik (Table 4, §3.3)**: at noise length 1000, $BS(1,2)$ reaches 97.80% ± 1.33% (margin +0.085 ± 0.070) and $A(\Gamma)$ reaches 97% ± 2.45 (margin +1.035 ± 0.088).

**Timing** (§3.1): generating and testing 1000 words of length 1000 in $BS(1,2)$ took "14.362 ± 0.316s seconds for generation and 0.181 ± 0.042 seconds for inference".

## Assumptions

- Training-distribution trivial words are short bases plus at most 150 local insertions. Evaluation words are drawn from the same generator family.
- $BS(1,2)$ labels come from a heuristic reduction (an upper bound on geodesic length). $A(\Gamma)$ labels assume the sorting system is confluent (see quality notes).
- One random Artin group (one seed); a single run configuration per task. Standard deviations come from 5 test repetitions, not from retraining.

## Limitations / scope

- "The outputs are strictly heuristic, and the evaluations were restricted to word lengths up to 20,000" (§4).
- The model is a classifier over lengths up to $L_{max} = 200$ and does not extrapolate past it (§3.2).
- A(Γ) evaluation is capped at length 1000 by memory cost of data generation (§3.1).
- Researcher's observations:
  - In-distribution tangling makes "trivial" detectable from local statistics. No adversarial or high-area trivial words are tested.
  - The "notoriously hostile" geometry of $BS(1,2)$ (§2.1.1) is never sampled. The area of a tangled identity word is at most its number of relator insertions, so at most about 150, far below $\exp(n)$ at $n = 5000$.
  - No certificate or reduction is produced; the output is an embedding similarity.

## Replication evidence

None. This is a recent single-author preprint with no external replication and no in-vault experiment. No code link is given in the paper.

## Why this paper matters

It is one of the few ML papers that train directly on trivial and non-trivial words of infinite finitely presented groups at long lengths, and it reports strong length generalisation (train ≤ 100, test up to 20,000). It sits next to [[petschack-2025-symmetric-group]], which it cites, as evidence that sequence/graph models can learn word equality when words come from local insertions.

Read against [[kapovich-2003-generic-case-complexity]], the results are what genericity predicts. The positives are short bases with local noise, and the negatives are coarse scrambles. Both live in the easy part of instance space, so high accuracy does not show that the model has learned the group's hard geometry. The paper's motivation (exponential Dehn function, cryptographic hardness) and its data generator are disconnected.

**Relevance to challenge-gen:** This is a direct example of the failure mode the project is meant to fix. Its trivial-word generator is *certified*, because the tangling trace is a product-of-conjugates proof. But it has *no hardness control*: area is bounded by the operation count (≤ 150), and Dehn-function hardness is cited but never measured. A challenge-gen benchmark would provide what WPNet lacks: trivial words with certified high area or high solver cost, plus near-miss non-trivial negatives (not 25–50% scrambles). WPNet-style models are natural baseline solvers to test against such sets, and the gap between Table 2 accuracy on tangled words and accuracy on hard certified words would be the measurable signal.

## Open questions surfaced

- How does WPNet accuracy depend on the **area** of trivial words, at fixed length? A generator with certified area (e.g. [[elder-2015-random-trivial-words]]-style trajectories biased toward high area) would test whether the model learned geometry or local statistics.
- Do the embeddings separate trivial words from **near-miss** negatives, e.g. words that are trivial in a finite quotient but not in the group (cf. $B_0(2,5)$ vs $B(2,5)$ in [[project-challenge-gen]])?
- Is the A(Γ) "canonical form" actually a normal form once braid relations are present? This needs a check against an independent solver (e.g. kbmag).
- Could a regression or ranking head on certified area serve as a learned hardness metric for generated challenges?
